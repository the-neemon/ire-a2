\subsection{Anti-gaming: serving-unavailable features and the leak we found}
\label{sec:q9}

\textbf{What the system declines to use.} \eb{} ships three lifetime totals per article (views,
page views, read time) summed over the whole collection period, so for any impression they
include the future and cannot exist when serving. We trained the same ranker on the same rows with
and without them. The difference is small next to what the honest system already earns:

In Assignment~1 the same totals were worth $+$0.042 to $+$0.075, more than our whole semantic
axis, because they were compared against systems with no popularity signal at all. Here they
compete with click counts restricted to the past, which capture most of the same information
legitimately. Cheating would buy \QnineDelta{}, while the behavioural axis is worth \EBGainTest{}
over stage one, roughly an order of magnitude more. Both arms of this comparison are the
10-feature model, so it prices the leak against that round rather than against the shipped 22.
End to end, the servable re-ranker (\EBFinalTest{} on test) far outscores stage one with lifetime
popularity added (\EBLeakyTest{}). \mind{} cannot run this comparison at all: its lifetime columns
are null for all 65,238 articles.

\textbf{Enforcement.} Two tests guard the boundary. One asserts that no click, exposure or
engagement value used for an impression comes from at or after its timestamp, on every split; the
other fails if any scoring module reads a lifetime column. Each was verified by injecting a
violation and watching it fail, after we found checks that passed without checking anything
(Section~\ref{sec:lessons}).

\textbf{The leak we found, and what fixing it did.} \eb{} ships its history in two blocks, each
describing clicks before \emph{that block} begins. Our feature builder merged both, letting the
later block overwrite the earlier for 11,658 users. Our val split is carved from the earlier
block, so val impressions were described by history from their own future:
\textbf{99.8\% of validation and 99.9\% of training impressions were affected}, while test was
clean at 0.0\% because it comes from the later block itself (Appendix~\ref{app:leak}).

We found it by chasing a metric discrepancy, not with a test: two implementations of the same user
vector scored 0.6473 and 0.5506, and making one reproduce both numbers with only the history
source changed confirmed the cause, a $+$0.0967 inflation on that feature. The fix reads one block
per split, and a new test that fails on injection covers it. Two consequences show the leak was
real and the fix was right. The read-time-weighted user vector's apparent $+$0.0085 contribution
fell to $+$0.0002, not significant, so its entire value had been the leak. And test AUC
\emph{rose} from 0.7429 to 0.7461, because test features were always clean and only the model,
now trained without future information, changed.
