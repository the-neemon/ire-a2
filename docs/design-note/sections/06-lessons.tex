\section{Observations that generalise}
\label{sec:lessons}

Five things surprised us enough to change how we work.

The first is that a feature's isolated score says little about its contribution. Impression size,
\feat{n_cands}, is identical for every candidate in an impression, so alone it scores exactly
0.5000, yet removing it costs $-$0.0066 test AUC, the second largest loss in the grid. It helps
only through interactions, by telling the model how crowded the impression is. The reverse also
happens: freshness carries high LightGBM split gain, but removing it changes almost nothing,
because popularity already holds the same information. We therefore quote ablation deltas, never
importance.

Leave-one-out deltas also fail to add up, which cost us more time than the rest combined. In the
17-feature grid, removing \feat{pop_causal} alone or \feat{user_read} alone makes the model
slightly better on val. Removing both is significantly \emph{worse} on both splits ($-$0.0005 val,
$-$0.0007 test), and keeping only the features with significant positive deltas loses 0.0240 test
AUC. Correlated features cover for each other, so a single-feature ablation answers ``what does
this add given the rest'' and not ``what should we keep''. We ship all features.

Our stage-one ablations first ran on the 10$\times$ smaller \eb{} demo set, where two intervals
spanned zero and we were ready to call both null. At full scale they resolved in opposite
directions: dropping the abstract from BM25 went from $+$0.0009 [$-$0.0062, $+$0.0081] to a real
$+$0.0025 [$+$0.0002, $+$0.0047], while Danish stemming stayed flat within $\pm$0.0004, at
intervals tight enough to exclude the $+$22 to $+$36\% recall gain Assignment~1 had claimed. An
underpowered result is not a null result, and we now re-run any interval spanning zero at larger
scale before recording a verdict.

The most uncomfortable finding concerns our tests. Six checks on this project passed while testing
nothing at all, the last of them an injection test that two functions sharing a name had silently
deleted, so it ran zero times from the day it was written. Reading the file cannot catch that;
counting collected tests can, and 12 collected against 15 written is what exposed it. Every test
must now fail on an injected violation before it counts, and how many actually run is part of what
we check. All six, and the related case of a guard that fired on a comment rather than on code, are
in Appendix~\ref{app:vacuous}.

Finally, when a number fails on exactly one dataset, the definition is a better suspect than the
code. Three of our metrics turned out to carry two definitions each, and every one reproduced on
one dataset and not the other, because the definitions diverge only when impressions carry several
clicks (28.8\% on \mind{} against 0.5\% on \eb{}). The three are in Appendix~\ref{app:names}; one
of them is how we found the leak.

\section{Limitations and open questions}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
\item Parts of Q1 are not modelled: no within-session features (\eb{}'s session id is
  unused) and no position-bias correction. Time decay on the history was neutral to harmful at
  every setting in Assignment~1 and was not revisited inside the ranker.
\item The baseline covers one dataset. NRMS was reproduced and beaten on \eb{} only. The
  history length of 100 our sweep favours was not tried in NRMS, whose cost grows quadratically
  with it.
\item The final model may be under-trained. Its best iteration is 399 of a 400-round cap,
  so it was still improving when training stopped; the reported gain is more likely understated
  than inflated.
\item Our strongest claims are about \eb{}. \mind{}'s gain is a small fraction of it, a
  property of the data rather than the method, but one dataset carries most of the evidence.
\end{itemize}
