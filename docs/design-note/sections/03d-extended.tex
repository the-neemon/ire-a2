\subsection{Slices and beyond-accuracy}
\label{sec:extended}

An average can hide a system that fails a subgroup, so we split the test impressions by how much
history the user has and by how often the clicked article was clicked in training.
\textbf{Slice sizes are printed because the thresholds are not obvious}: over 90\% of articles in
both datasets have zero training clicks, so a percentile over the whole catalogue would be zero
and would put every impression in the head slice. Taking it over articles seen in training gives a
head threshold of 349 clicks on \eb{} and leaves head at 803 impressions, 0.3\% of the split.

\textbf{The gain is uniform across the slices that matter}, with cold, warm and tail all improving
by about 0.25 AUC. \textbf{Cold and warm users are now indistinguishable} (0.7910 and 0.7908),
which is a property rather than an achievement: the model's strongest features are popularity and
exposure, which need no history, so having little history costs almost nothing.

\textbf{Head articles are the exception, and the result reverses across the split boundary.} On
validation the re-ranker beats fused retrieval on head (0.7080 [0.6905, 0.7253] against 0.6638
[0.6467, 0.6800]); on test it loses (0.5530 [0.5316, 0.5728] against 0.6353 [0.6143, 0.6574]).
Both differences have non-overlapping intervals, but a significant effect that flips sign across a
temporal boundary is a property of the window, not a stable claim. \textbf{So we cannot claim the
re-ranker helps on the most popular articles, and on test it is clearly worse there than retrieval
alone.} One untested explanation fits: our popularity features count only the last six hours and
are rank-normalised inside the impression, a deliberate ``trending now'' signal, and head articles
are the steadily popular ones it discounts. Consistent with that, the leaky arm with lifetime
popularity is the best system on head (0.6921), the only slice where it wins, and \mind{}, whose
popularity window is unbounded, goes the other way: 0.7884 against 0.6347 on its 860 head
impressions, its largest slice gain. \mind{}'s users also gain most where they have least, 0.6288
against 0.5125 on the 2,214 impressions with no history at all.

\textbf{The accuracy gain is not paid for in diversity or novelty} (Appendix~\ref{app:beyond}).
The re-ranker has the highest novelty of any system on both datasets and diversity close to
BM25's, so it is not collapsing onto a few popular stories. It does cost a little catalogue
coverage: 0.2051 against 0.2026 on \eb{} test and 0.0529 against 0.0494 on \mind{}, roughly 3 to
7\% fewer distinct articles ever surfacing. That is the real trade and it is small.

\textbf{Coverage is quoted without an interval, on purpose.} It counts \emph{distinct} articles
rather than averaging over impressions, and a bootstrap draws only about 63\% of the impressions,
so the articles only the missing ones would have surfaced simply vanish and every resample
undercounts. Our own attempt makes the bias visible: coverage on \eb{} test is 0.2067 for BM25
while the resampled spread is [0.1875, 0.1920], an ``interval'' that excludes the value it
describes. We report the point estimate and keep the spread as a diagnostic.
